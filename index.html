    How to use this calculator

    1. 1

      Add each wall as a section

      Pick a shape for every flat wall, gable end, slanted knee wall and ceiling plane in the room, then type in its measurements. Nothing is summed up by hand — each section is measured on its own terms.

    2. 2

      Measure the odd shapes correctly

      For a trapezoid take the bottom width, the top width and the vertical height. For a gable take the full base and the peak height measured straight up. For a sloped plane take the run, the rise or the angle in degrees.

    3. 3

      Subtract your doors and windows

      Add each opening once, using its rough size — a standard door is 3 × 7 ft and a standard window is 3 × 4 ft. These are deducted from the wall area before coats are applied.

    4. 4

      Set coats, coverage and waste, then buy

      Two coats is the default. Enter the coverage rate printed on your paint can (typically 350 sq ft per gallon) and add a 10% waste allowance. The result rounds up to real can sizes, and the formula panel shows every step.

    How to calculate paint for a slanted wall

    A slanted wall — a knee wall in an attic, the wall under a sloping ceiling — is a trapezoid: a long bottom edge, a shorter top edge, and a straight vertical height. Its area is the average of the two widths multiplied by the height:

    Trapezoid area Area = ½ × (bottom width + top width) × vertical height

    Worked example

    A knee wall with a 10 ft bottom edge, a 6 ft top edge and an 8 ft vertical height gives:

    ½ × (10 + 6) × 8 = ½ × 16 × 8 = 64 sq ft

    Add the flat wall below it as a rectangle, then the whole room is summed in one pass. Two coats of that 64 sq ft is 128 sq ft of coverage; at 350 sq ft per gallon that is 0.37 gallons before waste, so a single gallon of paint covers it with room to spare.

    How to calculate paint for a gable end

    A gable end is a triangle sitting on top of a rectangular wall. The area of a triangle is half of base × height, and the height is measured straight up from the base line to the peak — not along the slope:

    Triangle area Area = ½ × base × peak height

    Worked example

    A gable end with a 12 ft base and a 4 ft peak height gives:

    ½ × 12 × 4 = 24 sq ft

    Remember to add the rectangular wall beneath the gable as its own section — the triangle is only what sits above the ceiling line. With two coats, 24 sq ft becomes 48 sq ft of coverage, which is roughly 0.14 gallons of paint.

    How much paint for an L-shaped room?

    An L-shaped room has no single formula — but it never needs one. Split the room along the inside corner into two rectangles, measure each one separately, and add the areas. This is exactly what the L-shaped room paint calculator does for you: you add two rectangle sections and it does the summing, the coats and the can maths.

    Worked example

    A 14 × 12 ft room with a 6 × 4 ft corner cut out splits into a 14 × 8 ft rectangle (112 sq ft) and an 8 × 4 ft rectangle (32 sq ft):

    112 + 32 = 144 sq ft of paintable wall

    Two coats needs 288 sq ft of coverage. At 350 sq ft per gallon that is 0.82 gallons, and with a 10% waste allowance you are still inside one gallon. Split the room the other way and you get the same 144 sq ft — the total never changes, only the number of sections.

    Vaulted and slanted ceiling walls

    A vaulted or sloped ceiling is a plane tilted off horizontal, so it is longer along the slope than it is along the floor. Multiply the horizontal run by the slope factor to get the true surface length, then multiply that by the width of the plane:

    Sloped surface Surface length = √(run² + rise²)  or  run ÷ cos(angle)

    Worked example

    A ceiling plane with an 8 ft horizontal run and a 3 ft rise is 8.54 ft long along the slope:

    √(8² + 3²) = √73 = 8.54 ft  ·  12 ft wide = 102.5 sq ft

    If you know the angle instead of the rise, use run ÷ cos(angle) — the calculator accepts either and converts them for you.

    Frequently asked questions

    How do I calculate paint for a slanted wall?

    A slanted wall is a trapezoid: a long bottom edge, a shorter top edge and a straight vertical height. Add the two horizontal widths together, halve the sum, then multiply by the height — Area = ½ × (bottom + top) × height. A wall with a 10 ft bottom, a 6 ft top and an 8 ft vertical height gives ½ × (10 + 6) × 8 = 64 sq ft. Enter those three numbers as a Trapezoid section and the calculator shows you the complete slanted wall method with your own figures substituted.

    How much paint do I need for a vaulted ceiling?

    Measure the vaulted ceiling as a sloped plane. You need the room's length (wall to wall), the horizontal run from the top of the wall to the peak, and the rise from the top of the wall up to that peak. The sloping length is √(run² + rise²), or run ÷ cos(angle). With a 12 ft wide room, an 8 ft run and a 3 ft rise, one ceiling slope covers 12 × 8.54 = 102.5 sq ft. Add the matching gable ends as triangle sections and the total is what you feed into the coverage rate. The vaulted ceiling method has the full slope-factor table.

    How do I calculate paint for a gable end (triangular wall)?

    A gable end is a triangle sitting on top of a rectangular wall. The area of a triangle is half of base × height, where the height is measured straight up from the base to the peak — never along the slope. A gable with a 12 ft base and a 4 ft peak height is ½ × 12 × 4 = 24 sq ft. Measure the base across the full wall width, measure the peak up from that base line, and add the rectangle below the gable as its own section so the two areas are not double counted. The gable end method walks through it with a worked example.

    How much paint do I need for an L-shaped room?

    An L-shaped room is not a single formula — it is two rectangles. Split the room along the inside corner and measure each part separately. A 14 × 12 ft room with a 6 × 4 ft corner cut out becomes a 14 × 8 ft rectangle (112 sq ft) plus an 8 × 4 ft rectangle (32 sq ft) for 144 sq ft of wall in total. Add both as Rectangle sections: that is exactly the approach the L shaped room paint calculator automates, including coats, waste and rounding up to real can sizes. The L-shaped room method shows the full worked example.

    How do I measure a trapezoid-shaped wall for painting?

    You need three numbers: the bottom width where the wall meets the floor, the top width where it meets the sloping ceiling, and the vertical height from floor to the top of the wall. Measure the height plumb — straight up, not along the slope — and measure both widths horizontally with your tape level. Do not use the diagonal: the sloping length of a trapezoid is a different measurement from its height. Enter the three values as a Trapezoid section and the calculator computes ½ × (bottom + top) × height for you.

    Do I subtract doors and windows when calculating paint?

    Yes. Doors and windows are deducted from the total wall area before coats are multiplied in, because you never paint the glass or the door leaf itself. Add each opening only once, even if the same window sits on two different walls, and use its rough opening size rather than the exact frame size. A standard door is 3 × 7 ft (21 sq ft) and a standard window is 3 × 4 ft (12 sq ft). Subtracting openings typically saves 5–10% of the paint you would otherwise order.

    How many coats of paint should I calculate for?

    Two coats is the standard for most interior work: the first covers the old colour, bare patches or filler, and the second evens out the sheen. One coat is enough for a fresh, uniform surface or a repaint of the same colour in good condition. New drywall, a dramatic colour change, or a dark colour going over a light one usually needs a third coat. This calculator defaults to 2 coats and steps from 1 to 4 so you can test the difference.

    How much does one gallon of paint cover?

    A gallon of good quality interior latex typically covers 350–400 sq ft (32–37 m²) per coat, and 350 sq ft per gallon is the figure most paint calculators use. Primer and flat ceiling paint cover a little more; textured walls, porous plaster and roller loss push the number down. Always read the coverage rate printed on the can — it is the single biggest factor in your final order. Enter it in the coverage field and every number downstream recalculates instantly.

    How much extra paint should I buy for touch-ups and waste?

    Add 10% as a safe default. That covers touch-ups, patchy absorption on the first coat, roller and brush loss and spillage — the four reasons people end up short. Use 15–20% for heavily textured or very porous surfaces, or when painting a much darker colour over a light one. Use 0% only for a small touch-up job. Sealed leftover paint keeps for years, so a modest surplus costs far less than a second trip to the store.

    What is the formula for the area of a triangular wall?

    Area of a triangle = ½ × base × height. For a gable end the base is the full width of the wall at floor level and the height is the vertical distance from that base line up to the peak. A 12 ft wide gable with a 4 ft peak gives ½ × 12 × 4 = 24 sq ft. Multiply that by your number of coats, divide by the coverage rate on the can, and add your waste allowance. The calculator does all three steps and prints them line by line in the “How we calculated this” panel.

    How do I calculate paint for a room with slanted ceilings and dormer windows?

    Break the job into flat pieces and let the tool add them up. Each knee wall is a Trapezoid, each sloped ceiling plane is a Sloped rectangle, each dormer front is a Rectangle and each dormer roof is a Triangle. Then add every dormer window to the openings list so its glass area is deducted exactly once. The calculator totals all those shapes into a single paintable area, applies your coats and waste allowance, and shows the arithmetic for each piece so you can double-check every measurement you entered.

    Should I round up when buying paint?

    Always round up — you want more paint than the exact figure, never less. The calculator rounds to real can sizes (1 quart, 1 gallon and 5 gallons in the US; 1 litre and 5 litres in metric) and tells you how much you will have left over. If the exact need is 2.3 gallons, buy three gallons; if a 5-gallon can works out cheaper per gallon at your store, buy that instead and keep the difference for touch-ups.
